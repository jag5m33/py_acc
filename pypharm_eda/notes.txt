US ACCIDENTS (2016-2023) — FULL EDA WALKTHROUGH

what it means, and what it led to next.
================================================================================

Question:  What weather, time-of-day and road features best predict whether an
           accident will be severe, so authorities can target warnings and
           resources?
Target:    severity, 1-4, measuring the effect on traffic delay
Dataset:   Kaggle US Accidents, US_Accidents_March23.csv, 7,728,394 rows,
           46 columns

1.13  The four light columns and light_score
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
TESTED    Crosstabs of sunrise_sunset against astronomical_twilight, and

RESULT    sunrise_sunset Day / astronomical Night   = 0.00

MEANING   The four columns are nested, not independent. They rank by how strict

RESULT    light_score distribution: 0 (day) ~5.3m, 4 (full night) ~1.3m,

1.19  weather_condition
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
TESTED    Converted to lower case, stripped whitespace, counted unique values
          and cumulative coverage.

RESULT    145 distinct values, 144 after stripping.
          The TOP 13 cover 95% of all rows.
          The remaining 132 categories share the last 5%.

MEANING   A long tail of 132 categories is too sparse to estimate anything
          from reliably.

          More importantly, the column encodes FOUR INDEPENDENT THINGS in one
          text field:
             precipitation type   (rain, snow, sleet)
             intensity            (light, heavy)
             obscuration          (fog, haze, smoke)
             wind and thunder     (windy, blowing, thunder)

          One-hot encoding 145 categories would be wrong. The right approach is
          keyword-matched binary flags plus an ordinal intensity, which lets one
          row carry several facts at once.

TRAPS NOTED FOR THE KEYWORD MATCHING
             "funnel cloud" is a tornado precursor, not cloudiness
             "n/a precipitation" is a missing marker, not weather
             "in the vicinity" / "nearby" means weather NOT at the accident site
             "drifting" implies wind
             squalls are not thunder

RESULT    end_time        when the disruption cleared — no

################################################################################
STAGE 2 — RELATIONSHIPS: DOES THIS COLUMN CHANGE THE CHANCE OF SEVERITY?
################################################################################

METHOD
    Baseline = % severe across all rows. Whole dataset: 19.5%.
    Single-column test: filter to one value, recompute % severe, compare to
    the baseline.
    Split test: repeat that comparison INSIDE each value of a suspected
    confounder. In a split you compare the two cells in the same ROW of the
    table, not against the overall baseline.
        gap survives    -> real effect
        gap disappears  -> the link belonged to the other column
    At 3-7 million rows everything is statistically significant. Judge by the
    SIZE of the gap, never by p-values.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.2  Weather, first attempt (full dataset)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
TESTED    Keyword search on weather_condition, % severe per keyword group.

RESULT    overcast 34.95   clear 33.56   rain 23.35   snow 20.04
          haze 19.93       cloud 19.82   fog 13.07    fair 11.72

THE ANOMALY
          "clear" and "fair" describe the same sky. Gap: 21.8 points.
          "overcast" and "cloudy" describe the same sky. Gap: 15.1 points.

MEANING   Different weathers SHOULD differ — snow versus fog is a real
          difference and a gap there is a finding. SYNONYMS should not, because
          there is no weather difference between them, only a difference in
          wording. A 21.8-point gap between two words for the same condition
          cannot have been caused by weather.

          Analogy: two thermometers in one room reading 20C and 68F is fine —
          same temperature, different scale. One reading 20C and the other 35C
          means an instrument is broken. This is a check on the instrument.

SECOND WARNING
          snow came out at 20.04% against a 19.5% baseline — no effect at all.
          Snow having no influence on accident severity is not believable.

NEXT      Find what is attached to the choice of word.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.3  weather_condition crossed with source and year
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
TESTED    Crosstab of the four words against source and start_year.

RESULT    Source1 "clear":  58,195 (2018) -> 13,451 (2019) -> 0 from 2020
          Source1 "fair":      862 (2018) -> 99,455 (2019) -> 749,567 (2022)
          Same switch for overcast -> cloudy.
          Zero rows of clear or overcast after 2019 in ALL THREE sources.

MEANING   The vocabulary switches at the end of 2019, simultaneously across all
          three sources. That rules out the accident providers: if Source1 had
          changed its own wording, Sources 2 and 3 would be unaffected.

          A change hitting all three at once points at something shared — the
          WEATHER DATA SUPPLIER used to attach readings to accidents.

          So: "clear" effectively means "accident from 2016-2019"
              "fair"  effectively means "accident from 2020-2023"
          The 21.8-point gap was measuring WHEN, not WHAT.

NEXT      If those words mark eras and show different severity, severity itself
          must differ by era. Check immediately — it would affect every column.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.4  % severe by year  — THE TURNING POINT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
TESTED    groupby start_year, % severe.

RESULT    2016  34.2      2020  18.4
          2017  35.6      2021  11.4
          2018  35.6      2022   6.9
          2019  27.9      2023   2.9

MEANING   A TWELVEFOLD DECLINE. Flat for three years, then falling every year
          after. Accidents did not become twelve times less disruptive. The
          LABEL changed.

          Three consequences:
            1. The 19.5% baseline is an average over regimes that never
               coexisted. It is not a meaningful "typical" rate.
            2. Any column whose prevalence shifted over time inherits the year
               effect for free. Recent years hold far more rows, so anything
               more common recently looks artificially safe.
            3. A model trained on this learns "older record = severe" — a fact
               about the filing system, useless for prediction.

          The clear/fair anomaly was the first symptom. There would have been
          more.

NEXT      Decision point.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.6  DECISION — restrict to Source2
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
WHAT "GRADED DIFFERENTLY" MEANS
          Severity is not measured by an instrument. Someone at the reporting
          provider decides where the line falls between minor and serious
          delay. Each provider draws that line in a different place, and some
          moved it over time.

          Source2 draws it in a place that puts 33.92% of its accidents in the
          severe category, against 19.5% for the dataset as a whole. So Source2
          applies the severe label MORE readily. Not an error — a different
          convention.

WHY THEY CANNOT BE MIXED
          If Source1 calls 33% severe in 2018 and 2.7% in 2022, the word
          "severe" means two different things inside one column. Any feature
          correlated with source or year then picks up that meaningless signal
          and looks predictive when it is not.

WHY SOURCE2
          The only source whose rate holds steady across its whole window.
          3,305,373 rows = 43% of the dataset, far more than needed.
          Only one source remains, so source can no longer confound anything.

CONSEQUENCE
          New baseline: 33.92% severe. Everything computed before this point
          has to be re-run.

NOT PERMANENT
          Later options: re-test the main findings on Source1's own stable
          window (2016-2018) as a robustness check; or use all three with
          source and year as explicit controls.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.7  Coverage check — what the restriction cost
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
TESTED    State counts, year counts, road-type mix and missingness, Source2 vs
          the full dataset.

RESULT (a) GEOGRAPHY — all states present but nine effectively absent:
              SD 12 rows, ND 18, MT 56, WY 202, ID 229, WV 442, ME 477,
              VT 554, AR 1,406. All rural, mostly northern.

RESULT (b) TIME — Source2 covers 2016-2022, no 2023 at all.

RESULT (c) ROAD MIX — Local 51.3% in Source2 vs 44.4% in the full dataset.
              Interstate 21.7% vs 25.1%.

MEANING   Source2 has proportionally MORE local-road accidents, not fewer. That
          rules out an alternative explanation: Source2's 80.4% interstate
          severity is NOT because it is a highway-focused feed. It genuinely
          applies the severe label more readily across every road type. Which
          is also why the baseline is 33.92% rather than 19.5%.

RESULT (d) MISSINGNESS — worse in Source2 for two columns:
              precipitation  28.5% -> 47.0%
              wind_chill     25.9% -> 43.6%
              wind_speed      7.4% -> 10.8%
              light columns   0.30% -> 0.05%  (better)

              only 1,541 remain.

RESULT (e) end_lat and end_lng are 100% MISSING in Source2 — all 3,305,373
              rows. They only ever existed for Sources 1 and 3.

MEANING   precipitation at 47% missing is now a serious Stage 3 problem.
          The end coordinates do not matter — they were leakage anyway — but it
          means distance(mi) for Source2 rows must be derived some other way.

NEXT      Re-run weather on Source2 and see whether the restriction worked.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.8  Weather, second attempt (Source2) — THE VALIDATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
TESTED    Identical keyword test, Source2 only.

RESULT    Pair (same weather)   Full dataset   Source2
          clear vs fair         21.8 points    4.6 points
          overcast vs cloudy    15.1 points    0.4 points

MEANING   Synonyms now agree. The instrument is reading consistently, so
          differences between genuinely different weathers can be trusted.
          This is the proof that the Source2 decision was correct.

          The residual 4.6 points is explained: Source2 also spans the 2019
          switchover, and its own rate dipped from 36.8 to 30.3 around then.

RESULT    Against the 33.92% baseline:
              snow     44.23  (+10.3)
              rain     40.01   (+6.1)
              haze     37.79   (+3.9)
              overcast 34.93   (+1.0)  no effect
              cloud    34.48   (+0.6)  no effect
              clear    34.00   (+0.1)  no effect
              fair     29.42   (-4.5)
              fog      23.57  (-10.4)

MEANING   Snow and rain raise severity. Cloud cover does nothing, which makes
          sense — whether the sky is grey has no bearing on how a road behaves.

          Note how badly the blended data misled: it reported snow at 20.04%,
          meaning no effect. The true figure within a consistent source is
          44.23%, the largest weather effect there is.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.9  street_type
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
TESTED    % severe per road class, both datasets.

RESULT (Source2)   Local          14.67
                   County Road    17.60 approx
                   State Highway  23.73
                   US Highway     30.79
                   Other          36.84
                   Freeway        43.53
                   Unknown        49.72
                   Interstate     80.40

MEANING   A 66-point spread. The strongest single column in the dataset by a
          wide margin.

          It makes physical sense. A crash on a local street blocks one lane of
          a road with alternatives, at low speed. A crash on an interstate
          blocks a high-speed road with no way around it, so traffic backs up
          for miles. Severity measures disruption, so road class should
          dominate — and it does.

CONSEQUENCE
          Because it is this strong, any other column associated with road
          class will LOOK predictive when it is really just pointing at the
          road. street_type therefore becomes the column to SPLIT BY when
          testing everything else.

NOTE      "Unknown" at 49.72% sits between Freeway and Interstate, which is
          suspicious — unparsed street names behaving like major roads suggests
          street_type_shift is missing some highway naming patterns.

MEANING — THE SURVIVORS
          Six features keep a gap of 10+ points with road class held fixed. All
          six mark places where traffic is already slow and constrained — a
          light, a crossing, a stop sign, a roundabout, a shopping street, a
          bus stop. A crash there happens at low speed and blocks a short
          stretch. Severity measures disruption, so the outcome scores low.

MEANING — THE FAILURES
          give_way raw: 18.29 True vs 34.03 False, a 16-point gap.
          give_way within Local: 14.55 vs 14.68. Nothing.

          In the raw comparison the True group was almost all local-road
          accidents and the False group contained all the motorway accidents.
          It was never about give-way signs. It was local roads against
          motorways wearing a different label. bump behaves identically.

MEANING — THE REVERSAL
          traffic_calming looked protective raw (21.63 vs 33.94) but RAISES
          severity within local roads (18.24 vs 14.67). The direction flips,
          so the raw result was not merely inflated — it pointed the wrong way.

MEANING — JUNCTION
          Four times the rate on local roads. The largest single effect found
          anywhere in the analysis.

MEANING — INTERSTATE SATURATION
          Every one of the twelve leaves Interstate between 74% and 92% against
          an 80.40% baseline. Once an accident is on an interstate, nothing
          else moves the outcome. For interstate accidents, road class alone is
          effectively the whole prediction.

NEXT      Two questions raised: are the six survivors duplicates of each other,
          and is junction really slip roads?
